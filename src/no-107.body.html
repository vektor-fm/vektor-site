The PROOF page · the note and the checks

A claimed proof, checked by machine.

On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a short note saying he believes he has, working with Claude, found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. There is no paper: the proof is Lean code that a program checks step by step. Here is his note, what the checkers found, and why it is not peer reviewed yet. Every line below was read at its source; the dates say when.

The claim, word for word

"I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis."

On how it was made: "this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line". On what he released: "I decided to only present Lean", and he writes that he is "presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics".

What the hypothesis says, in one line

In 1859 Bernhard Riemann guessed that the prime numbers, which look random, follow a hidden order: every non-trivial zero of his zeta function sits on one line. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists it as a Millennium Prize Problem, "Formulated in Riemann's 1859 paper", and notes it has been "checked for the first 10,000,000,000,000 solutions". Checking cases is evidence, not proof; a proof has to cover all of them.

What the checkers found.

A Lean proof is code: a small trusted program, the kernel, re-runs every step from the axioms and accepts or rejects it. So a proof too long for a person to read can still be checked by machine, as long as the statement it proves is the right one.

The team behind Lean: certified

The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, in a joint post on 2026-11-21: "The statement of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct" (it is Mathlib's own RiemannHypothesis), "Alpöge's library compiles correctly", and two independent tools confirm that "the theorem proved in Alpöge's library is exactly Mathlib's statement". Their conclusion: "We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct". They put the library at "17 million lines of code".

leanprover-community.github.io: the statement

MIT: compiled independently

Andrew Sutherland (MIT) rebuilt it himself and keeps a public results page, last updated 2026-11-24 05:25 UTC (re-read 2026-11-25, unchanged):

  • "All 175,187 modules compile" (Lean v4.33.1, Mathlib v4.33.1), zero errors.
  • The Lean kernel replayed every module: 175,187 of 175,187, zero rejections.
  • Independently written kernels (nanoda, con-leche) accept the full exported proof, and so do Lean's own kernel and its paranoid kernel.
  • The five headline theorems depend only on the three standard axioms.
  • Comparator v4.33.0 reports "Your solution is okay!".
  • Still open on that date: "The lake comparator (v4.36.0) run is still in progress."

His scope note: the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis here means Dirichlet L-functions. Scientific American quotes him: "This is indeed an historic moment".

What it is not, yet.

Certified by machine is not the same as accepted by mathematicians.

Not peer reviewed

There is no paper to review: the note presents only the Lean code, and no human-readable account of the proof had been published when we checked on 2026-11-24. Peer review, and mathematicians understanding why it works, are still to come.

The prize is not paid

Clay's rules ask for a solution "published in a Qualifying Outlet", that "at least two years must have passed since publication", and "general acceptance in the global mathematics community". None of that can have happened yet.
