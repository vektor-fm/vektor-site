Which of the five
roles are you?

Boris Cherny, who created Claude Code, looked at his own team and saw five archetypes hiding under the old job titles. Not tied to "engineer" or "designer" or "PM" — tied to how you actually work. Below: all five, the mix a healthy product needs at each stage, and a prompt that reads your own git history and tells you which one you are, with the commits that prove it.

01 ·

The Prototyper.

Comes up with brand-new ideas. Churns out many of them. Most don't ship, and that's the job, not a failure.

What it looks like

Fast, cheap first drafts. A dozen directions in the time it takes most people to commit to one. The output is mostly throwaway, on purpose — a prototyper's job is to find the one idea worth someone else's time.

Every product that now looks obvious was, at some point, one of a prototyper's discarded ten.

02 ·

The Builder.

Quickly turns a prototype or idea into production-grade product or infra.

What it looks like

Takes the prototyper's rough sketch and makes it real: hardened, tested, shipped behind a flag or straight to users. Speed from idea to something that survives contact with real traffic is the whole skill.

A team of only builders ships fast and never questions what it's shipping.

03 ·

The Sweeper.

Cleans up the UI, simplifies the code and system, unships what shouldn't have shipped, optimizes performance.

What it looks like

Deletes more than they add. Kills the feature nobody uses, collapses three settings into one, makes the thing faster without adding anything visible. The least celebrated role and one of the most valuable once a product has any age on it.

Net negative lines of code is a sweeper's version of a good week.

04 ·

The Grower.

Takes a product that's already been built and iterates on it to improve product-market fit.

What it looks like

Lives on the same shipped surface for months, not because they can't move on but because that's where the compounding is: copy tests, onboarding tweaks, the funnel step that's quietly leaking users. Small changes, repeated, on something real.

A grower's commits touch old files, not new ones — and they touch the same old files, over and over.

05 ·

The Maintainer.

Owns a mature system to make it secure, reliable, fast and efficient as it scales.

What it looks like

The person a security bug or a dependency CVE lands on. Unglamorous by design: the win is nothing breaking, which is invisible right up until it isn't. Old files, careful diffs, low line-count changes that matter disproportionately.

"Most people are a mix of two roles, and sometimes three," Cherny wrote — these aren't a personality test, they're a texture in your own history.

Fig. 06 ·

The mix a product actually needs.

Cherny's own mapping — the reel that adapted this post for video skipped it, so here it is in full.

New, pre-product-market-fit

Needs people strong at Prototyper + Builder + Sweeper. Everything is still being found and thrown away; a team here that's mostly Growers and Maintainers has nothing mature enough yet to grow or maintain.

Growing, has found fit

Needs Builder + Sweeper + Grower, and some Maintainer. The product is real now — the job shifts from finding it to compounding it without it collapsing under its own growth.

Strong product-market fit

Needs Sweeper + Grower + Maintainer, and some Builder. Most of the value left is in not breaking what works, squeezing the parts that are inefficient, and iterating carefully rather than throwing new ideas at something that already works.

Which one are you, really?

Self-reported roles are unreliable — everyone thinks they're a builder. Your git history doesn't lie the same way. This prompt reads it and tells you.

What it does

Paste it into Claude Code in any repo you've worked in. It scopes to your own commits (by git email), reads read-only signals — commit sizes, net additions vs deletions, branch life, unmerged and reverted work, file age, keywords like fix/refactor/security/feat — and maps them to the five roles with an explicit rubric, not a vibe.

It never edits, commits, or pushes anything. Read-only, every time.

What you get back

A primary and secondary role with a rough percentage split, the 3-5 actual commits that drove the call, and one concrete way to get better at your own role using AI. It also tells you plainly: this is a heuristic read of one repo's history, not a psychometric test.
