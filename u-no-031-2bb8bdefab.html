AI Policy · The Read

Tax the
agents.

The US federal government runs on taxes tied to human work — 84% of everything it collects. An AI agent that replaces a $70,000 salary pays none of it. The obvious fix is to tax the agents. This is the full argument from NO. 031: the setup, the levy, and the two technical holes that make it much harder than the slogan — with every source linked at the bottom. As promised.

The setup · who pays for the government wages

The government runs on paychecks.

So here's the uncomfortable question: what happens if AI agents start doing a meaningful share of that work? An agent that replaces a $70,000 salary doesn't pay income tax, doesn't pay payroll tax, and doesn't buy lunch near the office. The company that deploys it gets more profitable — but corporate tax is the small pipe, and it's shrinking: corporate receipts fell 14.7% in FY2025 while individual receipts grew 9.5%.

Fig. 02 ·

the idea
The idea · count the agents, charge per head levy

Tax the agents.

The obvious answer. Bill Gates proposed exactly this in February 2017 — a "robot tax" where the robot that replaces a worker gets taxed at a similar level, funding retraining and care work. The European Parliament had considered the same idea and rejected it the day before, on the grounds it would stifle innovation.

The idea is back, now with AI-specific framing. A February 2026 paper proposes an "Agent Displacement Levy" — a graduated corporate tax on firms deploying autonomous AI agents in roles previously held by humans. Senator Bernie Sanders has pushed for excise taxes on automating technology. The agentic AI market was about $7.6 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $183 billion by 2033, so the base is growing fast.

A per-agent levy sounds clean. Two technical problems make it much harder than it sounds.

Fig. 03 ·

hole no. 1
Hole 1 · jurisdiction shopping reroute

An agent is an API call.

A factory robot is bolted to a floor in a jurisdiction. An AI agent is a network request. It can run from a server anywhere on earth, and rerouting it through a low-tax jurisdiction is a config change, not a relocation.

We've run this experiment before. The OECD's BEPS project (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) exists because digital businesses built on intangible assets shift profits easily — the assets relocate with a filing, not a moving truck. The workaround countries reached for was digital services taxes: crude levies of 2–5% on gross revenue from digital activity, taxing where the customers are because taxing where the "work" happens proved impossible. The UK's DST collected £800 million in 2024–25. The OECD's Pillar One — the coordinated fix that would allocate taxing rights to market countries — has been stuck in negotiation for years.

If the tax attaches to where the agent runs, the agent moves. If it attaches to where revenue is booked, you've rebuilt the DST fight.

Fig. 04 ·

hole no. 2
Hole 2 · unmeterable local inference local

The agents are going local.

A per-agent tax needs a count of agents. That works if inference flows through a handful of API providers who can report usage, like banks report interest.

It doesn't work when the model runs on your own hardware. Open-weight models — Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek — can be downloaded and run locally, and enterprises are doing it at scale, particularly in regulated sectors that want data on-premises. Red Hat's 2025 review reports open-source AI deployment surging and a large share of enterprise AI workloads now including a local inference component. Microsoft's Phi models run on a phone. There is no API bill, no usage log leaving the building, and no practical way for a tax authority to count "agents" on private servers.

You'd be trying to meter software execution — the thing tax systems have never been able to see.

Fig. 05 ·

on the table
01Tax the infrastructure — compute and data centers are physical: US states filed 300+ data-center bills in early 2026, many rolling back tax exemptions. A de facto tax increase on AI infrastructure.
02Shift to consumption — the Tax Foundation's answer: a VAT taxes AI-produced goods and services when someone buys them, wherever the model ran.
03Do nothing special — the IMF advised in 2024 against special AI taxes entirely, calling them hard to operationalize.
The point · slogan vs. engineering verdict

"Tax the agents" is a slogan.

Tax policy is an engineering problem, and this one has two failure modes — jurisdiction shopping and unmeterable local inference — that are visible in advance to anyone who knows how the technology works. The debate that matters isn't whether AI should contribute to the tax base. It's whether policymakers design for how agents actually run, or pass a law the technology routes around by lunchtime.

The serious proposals mostly route around both holes by taxing things that can't move or hide — see the sources below for the full paper trail.
