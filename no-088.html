The CHAIN page · the generation table, 1 to 80

One neutron, eighty generations.

A uranium-235 atom catches a neutron and splits. Each split frees two or three neutrons, a few escape or get absorbed, and about two go on to split the next atoms, so the number of splits doubles every generation. Here is that doubling, generation by generation, with the time and the energy beside it.

See the table ↓Where the figures come from ↓

Fig. 01 ·

The rule behind the table.

Pure doubling: every split leads to two more. A model of the idea, not a simulation of a real device.

1Splits in generation n = 2n-1. Generation 1 is the first split, generation 7 is 64, generation 52 is about 2.25 × 1015 (over two quadrillion), generation 80 is about 6.04 × 1023, a six followed by twenty-three more digits.
2About 10 nanoseconds a generation. One step of a chain reaction takes about one "shake", 10 ns, so 80 generations take about 0.8 µs: about a microsecond.
3About 200 MeV a split. That is about a tenth of a percent of the atom's mass turned into energy. The energy column adds up every split so far and converts it to tons of TNT.

Fig. 02 ·

The generation table, 1 to 80.

Highlighted rows are the three counts the film shows: generation 7, 52 and 80. Scroll sideways on a phone.

Look at the last rows: each generation releases as much energy as all the generations before it put together. Almost all of the energy arrives in the final few doublings, which is why it all lets go at once.

Fig. 03 ·

How it lines up with Trinity.

Trinity, 16 July 1945: the first nuclear detonation, a plutonium implosion bomb.

1Over 20,000 tons of TNT. The official yield is 21 kilotons; a 2021 re-analysis puts it at 24.8 ± 2 kilotons.
2About one gram of mass. E = mc2: one gram is about 21.5 kilotons, so 21 to 24.8 kilotons is about 0.98 to 1.15 grams.
3Eighty is a round number. On this pure-doubling count the total reaches about 9.3 kilotons by generation 80 and passes Trinity's 21 kilotons at generation 82: two more doublings, about 20 ns. The film and its source use eighty, as the atomic archive does; the table shows the exact sums.
4The cloud rose about 12 kilometres (7.5 miles, 12.1 km).
